LOCAL IMPACT

There's no cost-free way to close this gap

Amendment 3 removes roughly $300 million a year from Jacksonville's General Fund once fully phased in. That money has to come from somewhere — and every realistic option lands somewhere real: public safety, other city services, or the wallets of renters and small businesses.

Where Jacksonville's General Fund goes today

A $2.03 billion budget, in three pieces. Only one of them is genuinely flexible.

General Fund — FY26-27$2.03B
Public Safety — 52%
Debt & Pension — 14%
Everything Else — 34%

Public Safety — ~$1.05B

Jacksonville Sheriff's Office and Fire & Rescue combined.

Debt & Pension — ~$287M

Contractually locked obligations — not a choice in any given year.

Everything Else — $691M

Parks, libraries, public works, neighborhoods, and every other city service.

Three ways this could land

These aren't mutually exclusive — the real outcome is likely some mix of all three.

AT RISK
52%

Public safety cuts are a real possibility

Public safety is more than half of Jacksonville's General Fund — the single largest source of savings in the budget. A loss this size puts every part of spending in play, police and fire included.

  • Staffing, equipment, and response times all exposed
  • Not a hypothetical — the largest line item is the largest target
AT RISK
43%

Or dramatic cuts to everything else

Even if public safety were fully protected, closing the gap through every other service alone would mean eliminating roughly 43% of the $691 million that funds parks, libraries, roads, and neighborhood programs combined.

  • Deep reductions to library hours and locations
  • Road, sidewalk, and stormwater maintenance backlogs grow
COST SHIFT
Someone else pays

Or higher costs for renters and small businesses

Avoiding cuts altogether means raising replacement revenue instead — higher millage rates, fees, or sales tax. Those costs land hardest on rental and commercial property, and tend to get passed through as higher rent and prices.

  • Landlords facing higher tax bills tend to raise rent
  • Businesses facing higher tax bills tend to raise prices

A couple of quick questions

Would this raise my rent?

It's likely for many renters. If the city (or other taxing authorities) raise millage rates on rental property to replace lost revenue, landlords facing a higher tax bill generally pass at least some of that cost through rather than absorb it.

Would small businesses end up paying more?

Possibly, through the same mechanism — higher commercial property tax bills, or new/higher fees and sales tax used to offset the shortfall. Small businesses would face that alongside their own customers seeing less relief than headline "tax cut" framing suggests.
