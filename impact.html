LOCAL IMPACT

There is no version of this where nobody pays

Amendment 3 doesn't reduce what Jacksonville has to do. It reduces the money the city does it with. That leaves the city two honest options — provide less, or collect the money from somewhere else. Both of them reach you.

Where Jacksonville's General Fund goes today

The city's General Fund is about $2.03 billion, and it comes in three pieces. Only one of them is genuinely flexible in any given year.

General Fund — FY26–27 adopted$2.03B
Public safety — 52%
Debt & pension — 14%
Everything else — 34%

Public safety — about $1.05B

Jacksonville Sheriff's Office and Fire-Rescue combined. Already more than half the General Fund, and rising for reasons unrelated to this amendment — the JSO and Fire-Rescue pension is roughly 47% funded.

Debt & pension — about $287M

Bond payments and required pension contributions. Contractually locked — not a choice the city gets to make in a given year.

Everything else — about $691M

Parks, libraries, public works, neighborhoods, planning, economic development, health and human services, and general government.

FY26–27 adopted General Fund. Note this is the General Fund specifically, not the full consolidated city budget — the fund that pays for day-to-day city services and the one Amendment 3 draws from.

Two ways this lands — and the city has to pick

These aren't mutually exclusive; the real outcome is likely a mix. But there is no third path where the money simply isn't needed.

Path one — provide less

Cut services until the books balance

Debt and pension obligations can't be touched, so cuts come from public safety, from everything else, or from both. Protecting police and fire entirely means the whole reduction falls on the smaller share of the fund — the part that runs the parks, the libraries, the road crews, the code enforcement officers and the neighborhood programs.

  • Library hours and branch locations
  • Road, sidewalk and stormwater maintenance, already backlogged
  • Park upkeep, community centers and recreation programs
  • Code enforcement, animal care and resident services

Jacksonville spends less per resident than any of fourteen peer cities — about $2,065. This is not a budget with an obvious layer of fat to remove first.

Path two — collect it elsewhere

Raise the money from someone else

Avoiding cuts means finding replacement revenue — a higher millage rate, new or higher fees, or a sales surtax. Millage increases fall hardest on rental and commercial property, and those costs are generally passed along rather than absorbed. The people who never received the homestead break end up helping to pay for it.

  • Renters, through rent that reflects the landlord's higher tax bill
  • Customers, through prices that reflect a business's higher tax bill
  • Small businesses, absorbing what they can't pass on

Amendment 3 also drops the non-homestead assessment cap from 10% to 5% a year — slowing the natural revenue growth that might otherwise have covered part of the gap, and pushing harder toward an explicit rate increase.

Why public safety can't quietly absorb it

Public safety is already more than half of the General Fund, and its costs are rising independently of this amendment: the JSO and Fire-Rescue pension is roughly 47% funded, which requires a contribution exceeding 100% of covered payroll. Whatever else is true, this is not a part of the budget with room to take on additional pressure.

What you would actually notice

Cuts of this scale don't show up as a line in a budget document. They show up as the ordinary things that stop working as well.

The branch library closer to closing

Reduced hours first, then programming, then locations. Branches are among the easiest things to trim and among the most used.

The pothole that stays a pothole

Road, sidewalk and drainage maintenance is deferrable, which is exactly why it gets deferred — onto an already substantial backlog.

The park that stops getting mowed

Grounds upkeep, community center hours and recreation programs are discretionary in a way that police and fire are not.

The code complaint nobody answers

Code enforcement, animal care and resident service requests all run on staff counts, and staff counts are where cuts land.

The rent that goes up anyway

If the city raises millage instead of cutting, renters see the cost without ever having received the exemption.

The slower storm recovery

Debris removal and cleanup capacity is drawn from the same public works budget as everyday maintenance.

Questions people are asking

Would this raise my rent?

It's plausible for many renters. Renters get nothing directly from a homestead exemption, since it applies only to owner-occupied homes. If the city or another taxing authority raises millage on rental property to replace lost revenue, a landlord facing a higher tax bill generally passes at least part of it through rather than absorbing it.

Would small businesses end up paying more?

Possibly, through the same mechanism — a higher commercial property tax bill, or new and higher fees and sales tax used to offset the shortfall. Small businesses would face that at the same time their customers discover the headline relief was narrower than advertised.

Can't the city just find the savings in waste?

Jacksonville already spends less per resident than any of fourteen comparable cities. A reduction of this size is not the scale of thing that gets absorbed by trimming inefficiency; it is the scale of thing that removes services.

Doesn't the state make up the difference?

Not for the non-school portion, which is what Amendment 3 changes. School taxes are excluded from the exemption increase; the revenue this removes is city revenue, and replacing it is a city problem.
