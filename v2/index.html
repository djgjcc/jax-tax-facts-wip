Three things every Jacksonville voter should know

01

Jacksonville already invests less in its residents than peer cities — this would make it worse

Among fourteen comparable cities, Jacksonville spends the least per resident. There is no pool of obvious waste standing by to absorb a cut of this size.

02

The savings come out of the fund that pays for police, fire, parks and libraries

Amendment 3 doesn't reduce what the city has to do. It reduces the General Fund the city does it with — the same account that covers JSO, Fire-Rescue, road crews and branch libraries.

03

That leaves two options, and both cost you something

Either services shrink, or the city raises the money somewhere else — through millage, fees or sales tax that land on renters, consumers and small businesses. There is no third, free option.

What Amendment 3 actually does

Four changes to how property is taxed — and what each one sets in motion.

Raises the homestead exemption on non-school taxes from $50,000 today to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028.

SoMost of the revenue loss comes from here — and it is permanent, not a one-year dip the city can ride out.

New residents wait 5 years before qualifying for the higher exemption.

SoTwo neighbors in identical houses can owe very different amounts, based only on how long they've lived in Florida.

Lowers the non-homestead assessment cap on rental and commercial property from 10% to 5% a year.

SoRevenue from rental and commercial property grows more slowly too — closing off the one source that might otherwise have grown into the gap on its own.

Needs 60% voter approval in the November 2026 election.

SoIt is written into the state constitution if it passes — Jacksonville cannot opt out, and undoing it takes another statewide vote.

Who actually gets the tax break?

Amendment 3 is presented as relief for homeowners. It is worth knowing how narrowly that relief is distributed — because everyone shares the cost of it either way.

~14,000

Jacksonville homes already pay no property tax at all. A larger exemption gives them nothing further.

~47%

of Duval parcels see no direct homestead savings — including every rented home in the city.

$267–$2,238

Annual savings range for homes that do benefit. The larger figures go to the higher-value homes.

Renters get the cost without the break

The homestead exemption applies to owner-occupied homes. If you rent, you receive nothing directly — while any millage increase, fee or sales-tax change used to replace the lost revenue reaches you the same as everyone else.

That is the part most easily missed about a measure sold as putting money back in your pocket: whose pocket, and whose pays for it.
